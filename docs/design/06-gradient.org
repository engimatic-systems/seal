#+title: The Gradient: Accounting for the Cost of Judgment

* Scope

This note draws on [[https://porcuquine.github.io/the-gradient.html]["The Gradient"]].
Its role in Seal is narrow: a review boundary is an intervention in a human
system of incentives, habits, time pressure, interfaces, and persuasion. It is
not an escape from that system.

The [[file:05-cold-cut.org][Cold Cut]] states a structural condition: the agent cannot
write the host-side state through which a proposal becomes local execution. The
Gradient adds a human-factors condition: the operator who controls that state is
still influenced by the world, including by the proposal itself.

* No outside position

Seal must not promise an uninfluenced or omniscient reviewer. An agent can make
a persuasive proposal. An operator can be tired, hurried, optimistic, afraid,
or habituated to approval. A polished interface can bias a decision too. None
of this makes the Cut useless. It names the Cut's limit.

The point of the boundary is not to purify judgment. It is to ensure that an
agent cannot directly alter the authority-bearing transition after it has made
its case. Human judgment remains a real, situated act rather than a hidden
implementation detail.

* Accounted loss

Review costs time and attention. Refusal costs delay, work, and sometimes
social or operational friction. Recovery after uncertainty costs investigation.
These costs are not evidence that the boundary has failed. They are part of the
price of acting responsibly under uncertainty.

Seal should neither worship that cost nor hide it. The useful distinction is
between /accounted/ and /exported/ loss:

| Cost | Accounted form | Exported form |
|-
| Code review | Operator sees the full proposal and spends time deciding. | System replaces review with an opaque summary or silent auto-approval. |
| Delivery | Host retries result publication without rerunning code. | Failed publication silently causes duplicate execution. |
| Uncertainty | Started-but-unresolved work becomes =unknown= for manual resolution. | System converts uncertainty into an automatic retry or apparent success. |
| Feature complexity | Capability has explicit invariants, invalidation, and recovery. | Complexity becomes hidden interaction cost for a later operator or victim. |

Automation is good when it removes clerical cost while preserving awareness and
attribution. It is dangerous when it makes a decision feel free by moving its
uncertainty somewhere less visible.

* Product implications

Seal should prefer controls and metrics that keep this expenditure legible:

1. Make approval and refusal equally real actions; refusal must have a useful,
   attributable path back to the agent.
2. Do not use default approval, hidden confirmation, success-biased rendering,
   or throughput alone as a measure of system quality.
3. Show relevant evidence before a consequential decision, not only after
   something has failed.
4. Preserve pauses, inspection, and explicit =unknown= outcomes rather than
   translating them into invisible automation.
5. Treat every new convenience as a question: whose time, uncertainty, and
   recovery burden did it remove, and where did those costs go?

These are not a demand for maximal friction. They distinguish the cost of
awareness from accidental toil. Host-initiated sync, bounded output capture,
durable result delivery, and good review ergonomics should reduce the latter.
They must not make the former disappear by changing the authority contract.

* A small curvature

Seal is not a mechanism for controlling an agent or leaving the surrounding
gradient. It is a small, deliberate curvature in the path from language to
local consequence. It asks the chain to stop at a place where a human can see
what is being spent, decide whether to spend it, and make that decision bind.
